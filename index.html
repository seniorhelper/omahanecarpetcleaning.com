01The premise

Why fiber comes first

Your carpet is a
material, not a color

Two carpets that look identical can behave like completely different substances. Acid-dyed nylon has open dye sites that artificial food colorants bond to permanently, but it releases oily soil well and recovers its shape year after year. Polyester and triexta barely stain from a spilled sports drink, yet they hold grease so tightly that a dropped slice of pizza is the harder problem. Olefin will not dye-stain at all and crushes flat under traffic. Wool is a protein, and the alkaline products that clean synthetics beautifully will yellow and weaken it. One method applied to all five produces one good result and four compromises.

Consequence one

Wrong chemistry sets stains

Heat on a protein spill cooks it into the fiber the way an egg white sets in a pan. High alkalinity on wool causes yellowing and permanent protein binding. Neither is reversible, and both happen most often when somebody reached for a general-purpose spray without asking what the stain or the carpet actually was.

Consequence two

Residue re-soils the carpet

Most soil is acidic, so most pre-sprays are alkaline. If that alkalinity is not neutralized by an acid-side rinse it stays in the pile as a tacky film that grabs dirt from every footstep. This is the entire explanation for carpet that looks worse a month after cleaning than it did the week before.

Consequence three

You get sold the wrong fix

A spot that is soil comes out. A stain that is a bonded dye does not, and no second cleaning changes that. Knowing which one you are looking at before work begins is the difference between a fair invoice and paying twice for the same square foot with the same disappointing result.

02Cross section

Core sample

What a carpet
looks like sideways

Homeowners think of carpet as one thing. It is five, stacked, and a spill travels down through all of them. Where a contamination stops determines whether it is a twenty-minute fix or a construction job. Select a layer to see what lives there and what it takes to clean it.

Section A–A · residential cut pile
Layer 01 · 100% of what you see

Face fiber

The pile. This is the only layer anyone looks at and the only one most cleaning actually reaches. Dry particulate soil sits at the base of the tufts where it grinds against the fiber every time somebody walks past, which is why traffic lanes go dull before they go visibly dirty — that is abrasion, not dirt.

Everything above the backing is recoverable with the right chemistry. If the contamination stopped here, you have a routine cleaning and nothing more.

Layer 02 · primary, latex, secondary

The backings

Tufts are stitched through a primary backing, locked with a latex adhesive, then bonded to a secondary backing. This sandwich is where a spill becomes a repeat customer: liquid that soaks past the fiber sits in the latex, and cleaning the surface leaves it there.

As the carpet dries, that residue travels back up by capillary action and the spot reappears two days later looking untouched. Cleaners call it wicking. The fix is deeper extraction of that one area, not another surface pass over the whole room.

Layer 03 · the part nobody inspects

Cushion

Usually bonded urethane foam, and it behaves like a sponge. A pet accident that looked like a saucer on the surface is often a dinner plate down here, because liquid spreads sideways once it hits the pad and has nowhere else to go.

Odor that returns on humid Omaha afternoons almost always lives in this layer. Cleaning the carpet above it accomplishes nothing you can smell. It needs flooding and sub-surface extraction, or in bad cases the affected section cut out and replaced.

Layer 04 · past the point of cleaning

Subfloor

Plywood, OSB or slab. Once contamination reaches the deck, cleaning is over as a category — there is nothing to extract from because the liquid is inside the wood.

The honest repair is pulling the carpet back, replacing the pad section, sealing the subfloor with a shellac-based primer, and re-laying. Any company that quotes you a deodorizing treatment for a subfloor problem is selling you a scented delay, and the smell will be back before the invoice clears.

03Soil load

Composition

What is actually
in an Omaha carpet

Carpet works like a filter that nobody changes. It captures what comes in on shoes, paws and air, holds it at the base of the pile, and releases a portion back into the room with every footstep. The mix is specific to this metro.

Roughly three quarters

Dry particulate

Grit, sand, wind-deposited loess silt, skin cells, fiber fragments and pollen. It is abrasive, it sinks to the base of the tuft, and it is the reason vacuuming is the first extraction step rather than a courtesy. Water applied to dry soil does not remove it, it makes mud and drives it toward the backing.

The binding fraction

Oily residues

Cooking aerosol drifts out of the kitchen and settles across the whole floor as a fine film, joined by body oils, pet oils and tracked-in asphalt. This is what makes soil stick instead of vacuuming away, and it is why traffic lanes near a kitchen darken faster than anywhere else in the house.

Winter, November to March

Chloride ice-melt

Every Omaha driveway, garage floor and parking lot deposits salt on the first six feet of carpet inside each door. It dries to a white bloom, it is hygroscopic so it keeps drawing moisture, and it grinds like glass. It is also water-soluble, so it extracts cleanly when it is actually extracted rather than merely wetted.

Biological

Dander and dust mite matter

Dust mite fecal particles and pet dander are the clinically significant allergens in residential carpet, and they concentrate in bedrooms rather than living rooms. Extraction removes a large share of the reservoir. It does not replace a sealed-path HEPA vacuum or keeping indoor humidity in the 40 to 50 percent band.

05Diagnosis

Stain triage

Twenty stains,
six categories

A stain is not defined by what it looks like, it is defined by what it is made of. Coffee and cola look alike on beige carpet and need opposite treatments. Pick what happened and you get the chemistry, the correct first move, the mistake that sets it permanently, and an honest answer about whether this is worth your Saturday or worth a phone call.

Tannin stain

Coffee on carpet

Chemistry
Acid side. Tannin is a plant dye and responds to an acidic detergent, then an oxidizer if color remains.
Do now
Blot immediately with a white towel, rinse with cool water, blot again. Warm water helps on tannin once the bulk is lifted.
Avoid
Do not reach for an alkaline all-purpose spray first — it can drive the tannin deeper and darken it.
Verdict: DIY if fresh. Coffee with cream is a combination stain and behaves like tannin plus protein, which is a two-step job.
Tannin stain

Tea on carpet

Chemistry
Acid side, same family as coffee but usually lighter and more diffuse.
Do now
Blot, rinse cool, then treat with a mildly acidic solution. Patience beats pressure.
Avoid
Do not scrub. Tea spreads easily and scrubbing turns a spot into a halo.
Verdict: DIY if fresh. Older tea marks usually need an oxidizer and a test patch first.
Tannin stain

Red wine on carpet

Chemistry
Tannin plus natural pigment. Oxidizers break the color bonds; this is the classic case for oxidize organics.
Do now
Blot hard, dilute with cool water, blot again. Repeat before applying anything else — dilution does most of the work.
Avoid
Skip the salt pile and the white-wine folklore. Salt absorbs some liquid and leaves a gritty residue that has to come out later.
Verdict: Call a pro on light nylon. Red wine on a pale acid-dyed nylon is the most common permanent stain we are asked to fix.
Tannin stain

Fruit juice on carpet

Chemistry
Tannin if it is real fruit, dye if it is artificial. Grape and cranberry sit in both camps.
Do now
Blot, dilute, blot. Identify whether the color is natural or added before treating further.
Avoid
Do not apply heat. If there is artificial colorant in it, heat will help it bond.
Verdict: DIY if fresh and natural. Artificial-colored juice on nylon is a dye problem and needs professional reduction.
Protein stain

Blood on carpet

Chemistry
Cold water and enzyme. Protein coagulates with heat and then it is in the fiber for good.
Do now
Cold water only, blot from the outside in, repeat. An enzyme product with real dwell time finishes it.
Avoid
Never use hot water, a steam iron or a hairdryer. Heat sets protein permanently.
Verdict: DIY if fresh. Dried blood on a light carpet is worth a professional pass with a peroxide-based approach and a test patch.
Protein stain

Milk and dairy on carpet

Chemistry
Enzyme. The odor problem is bacterial and it arrives days after the visible mark is gone.
Do now
Blot, rinse with cool water, then apply an enzyme cleaner and give it the full dwell time on the label.
Avoid
Do not consider it done because it looks clean. Untreated dairy protein in the pad sours within a week.
Verdict: DIY if you catch it. A spilled sippy cup that soaked into the pad is a sub-surface extraction job.
Protein stain

Vomit on carpet

Chemistry
Protein plus acid. It can bleach carpet dye on contact, so speed matters more than technique.
Do now
Lift solids with a spoon, blot, flush with cool water, then enzyme. Ventilate the room.
Avoid
No heat, and do not let it sit overnight. Stomach acid discolors some dyes within hours.
Verdict: Call a pro if it has been more than a day or if it soaked in. Color loss cannot be cleaned back.
Protein stain

Pet urine on carpet

Chemistry
Enzyme, specifically uricase. Uric acid crystals are not water-soluble, which is why washing removes the stain but not the smell.
Do now
Blot heavily, weight a dry towel on it for hours to pull moisture out of the cushion, then use a true enzyme product.
Avoid
Do not use an ammonia-based cleaner, which smells like a marker to the animal and invites a repeat in the same spot.
Verdict: Call a pro for anything repeated. Surface treatment on a pad-level problem is money spent on a smell that returns.
Oil & grease stain

Cooking grease on carpet

Chemistry
Solvent then detergent. Water alone will not touch it because oil and water do not mix.
Do now
Scrape gently, apply a solvent spotter to a towel rather than the carpet, then blot and rinse thoroughly.
Avoid
Do not soak with water first — it spreads the oil ring wider without lifting anything.
Verdict: DIY on nylon. On polyester, triexta or olefin this is genuinely hard and worth a professional.
Oil & grease stain

Makeup and lipstick on carpet

Chemistry
Oil plus pigment. Two problems that need two steps in the right order.
Do now
Solvent first to break the oil, blot, then address any remaining color separately.
Avoid
Do not rub. Pigment spreads fast and a small mark becomes a smear.
Verdict: DIY for a small mark. Foundation ground into a light carpet is a professional job.
Oil & grease stain

Candle wax on carpet

Chemistry
Physical then solvent. The bulk comes out mechanically, the residue chemically.
Do now
Freeze it with a bag of ice, crack and lift the solid, then treat the oily shadow with a solvent.
Avoid
Do not use the iron-and-paper-bag method on synthetic carpet — the melting point of olefin is low enough that you can fuse the pile.
Verdict: DIY for the wax. Call a pro if the candle was dyed, because the color left behind is a dye stain.
Oil & grease stain

Tar and asphalt on carpet

Chemistry
Solvent. Common in Omaha in summer when a sealcoated driveway is fresh.
Do now
Lift what you can, then use a dedicated tar spotter applied to a towel, working the edges inward.
Avoid
Do not use gasoline or lighter fluid. Beyond the obvious risk, they can delaminate the backing.
Verdict: Call a pro. Tar spreads under pressure and this is the stain most often made worse by a first attempt.
Dye stain

Sports drink and punch on carpet

Chemistry
Reducing agent plus heat transfer. Oxidize organics, reduce synthetics — this is a synthetic.
Do now
Blot up everything you can with cool water immediately. That is the whole DIY protocol.
Avoid
Do not apply an oxygen bleach hoping to lift it. Artificial dye usually needs reduction instead.
Verdict: Call a pro on nylon. Red dye on acid-dyed nylon bonds to the same sites the mill used and needs professional dye removal.
Dye stain

Ink and marker on carpet

Chemistry
Solvent, and which solvent depends on whether it is ballpoint, gel or permanent.
Do now
Blot, never spread. Test any solvent on a hidden area, then apply to a towel and dab.
Avoid
Do not use hairspray. That advice predates modern formulations and now mostly adds a sticky resin.
Verdict: Call a pro for permanent marker. Some inks are effectively a dye and cannot be removed without color loss.
Dye stain

Hair dye on carpet

Chemistry
Dye on dye. The hardest category, because the product was engineered to bond permanently to protein.
Do now
Blot immediately with cool water and stop there. Everything else risks spreading it.
Avoid
Do not experiment with bleach. You will remove the carpet color along with the stain.
Verdict: Call a pro immediately. On wool this is often permanent and the honest answer may be a bonded insert.
Dye stain

Mustard on carpet

Chemistry
Turmeric is a natural dye and it is stubborn. Behaves as dye plus tannin.
Do now
Scrape, blot, dilute. Then leave it alone until it can be treated properly.
Avoid
Do not use an alkaline cleaner, which turns turmeric a deeper red and makes it worse.
Verdict: Call a pro. Mustard is the food stain most often mistaken for easy.
Water-soluble stain

Mud and loess silt on carpet

Chemistry
None needed if you wait. This is mechanical, and Omaha's fine silt soils are the local version.
Do now
Let it dry completely, then vacuum the solids. Treat only whatever shadow remains with a mild detergent.
Avoid
Do not touch it wet. Wiping wet mud drives fine silt into the backing and triples the area.
Verdict: DIY, reliably. This is the one stain where doing nothing for a few hours is the correct first step.
Water-soluble stain

Soda and sugary drinks on carpet

Chemistry
Water-soluble, but the sugar residue is the real issue.
Do now
Rinse with cool water and blot repeatedly until the pile no longer feels tacky underfoot.
Avoid
Do not stop at the color. Sugar left in the fiber is sticky and becomes a permanent dirt magnet.
Verdict: DIY if clear. A dark cola on light carpet has caramel color in it and behaves partly as a tannin.
Mineral stain

Ice-melt salt on carpet

Chemistry
Water-soluble mineral. Extraction, not chemistry, is what removes it.
Do now
Vacuum the dry crystals first, then flush with warm water and extract thoroughly.
Avoid
Do not use a rental machine that lays down more water than it recovers — the salt redissolves and wicks back as a white bloom.
Verdict: Call a pro for entry lanes after a hard winter. This is the single most common Omaha-specific job we run in March.
Mineral stain

Rust on carpet

Chemistry
Acid side. Rust is a metallic oxide and it needs an acidic remover, the opposite of most carpet chemistry.
Do now
Identify the source — a bed frame foot, a radiator, a planter — and remove it before treating anything.
Avoid
Do not use an oxygen bleach. It does nothing for rust and can set it while damaging surrounding dye.
Verdict: Call a pro. Rust removers are strongly acidic and will strip carpet color if left too long.

Always test any product on a hidden area first, never mix two products, and treat an unidentified carpet as wool until you know otherwise.

06Chemistry

The pH story

Oxidize organics,
reduce synthetics

That six-word rule from the professional cleaning trade settles most arguments about spot removal. Organic material — coffee, tea, wine, juice, food — responds to oxygen-based chemistry that breaks the color bonds. Artificial colorants respond to a reducing agent instead. Reaching for the wrong one wastes an afternoon at best and sets the stain at worst.

pH 0–6 · acid side

Acids

Dissolve minerals and tannins. This is the side you want for rust, hard-water residue, and coffee or tea marks. It is also the side of the final rinse: an acid-side rinse neutralizes the alkaline pre-spray so nothing sticky stays in the pile.

pH 7 · neutral

Neutral

Where wool and other protein fibers want to live, and where any carpet you cannot identify should be treated. Neutral products clean less aggressively, which is precisely the point when the alternative is permanent fiber damage you cannot see until it yellows.

pH 8–14 · alkaline side

Alkalines

Cut grease, oil and protein, which is most household soil, so most pre-sprays live here. The catch is that alkalinity left behind is tacky and attracts dirt, and on wool it causes yellowing and protein damage that no amount of later cleaning reverses.

Rules that hold on every fiber

  • Blot with pressure, never rub — rubbing distorts pile permanently
  • Work from the outside edge toward the center, or you create a ring
  • Cold water is the safe default when you do not know what the stain is
  • Dilution does most of the work; chemistry is the last ten percent
  • Give any product its full label dwell time — enzymes especially need minutes, not seconds
  • Rinse whatever you apply, or the residue becomes next month's dirt magnet

Things that quietly cause damage

  • Heat on protein — blood, milk, urine, vomit — sets it into the fiber for good
  • Oxygen bleach on wool, which weakens and yellows the protein structure
  • Mixing two products, which can neutralize both or generate something you should not breathe
  • Ammonia on pet urine, which smells like a territorial marker and invites a repeat
  • Over-wetting with a rental machine that cannot recover what it puts down
  • Scrubbing a dye stain, which spreads the colorant into clean fiber around it
07By location

Room by room

Every room fails
in its own way

Soil does not distribute evenly. About eighty percent of what enters a house stays within the first few feet of a door, and the rooms people worry about are rarely the rooms that need the work.

Highest soil load in the house

Entries and mudrooms

The first six feet inside every door takes chloride salt all winter and loess mud every spring. This is where a walk-off mat earns more than any product you can buy — a proper mat inside and out removes a large share of what would otherwise reach the rest of the floor. Clean these lanes more often than the rooms behind them.

Fastest wear per square foot

Stairs

Every tread takes a person's full weight driven into the nose of the step, which grinds grit into the fiber at exactly the point with the least support underneath. Stairs wear out years before the rooms they connect. They are also cleaned by hand tool rather than a wand, which is why they are priced by the flight rather than by the room.

The Omaha-specific problem

Basements and below-grade rooms

Cool foundation walls, limited airflow and summer humidity mean a basement can take twice as long to dry as an identical room upstairs. The right approach is lower moisture, a dehumidifier rather than an open window, and air movers until the pile is dry well below the surface. If the room has ever taken storm water, that is a different job and we need to know first.

Where the allergens actually are

Bedrooms

Dust mite matter concentrates where people sleep, not where they entertain, so the bedroom carpet nobody thinks about is usually the one affecting somebody's morning congestion. Bedrooms look clean because they see little traffic. Looking clean and being clean are different measurements.

Invisible and everywhere

Kitchen-adjacent carpet

Cooking aerosol travels further than people expect and settles as an oily film across the nearest carpeted rooms. That film is what makes dry soil stick instead of vacuuming away, so the dining room darkens faster than the guest room despite similar traffic. It needs solvent-side help, not more scrubbing.

Documented, not just cleaned

Rentals and turnovers

Clean after the movers, not before, because empty units clean faster and cost less. What matters as much as the result is the paperwork: an itemized invoice naming rooms and method is the document that settles a deposit dispute and satisfies a carpet warranty claim. We issue one per unit.

08Timing

Twelve months

The Omaha
carpet calendar

Cleaning at the right moment is worth more than cleaning more often. This is what the year actually does to a floor in eastern Nebraska.

January

Peak salt

Chloride residue accumulates in entry lanes daily. Vacuum entries twice a week; dry indoor air means anything cleaned now dries fast.

February

Grit build-up

Sand and cinders tracked from lots and sidewalks work to the base of the pile. Still the cheapest month to book — nobody else is calling.

March

Thaw and mud

Snowmelt turns yard soil to paste that arrives on shoes and paws. Salt from three months of winter is still in the fiber.

April

Best month of the year

Clean now and you remove an entire winter at once. Tree pollen is settling into the pile, so this also resets the allergen load before summer.

May

Grass pollen

Windows open, pollen in. Good month for households with an allergy sufferer to run their second cleaning of the year.

June

Humidity begins

Drying slows. Start running the HVAC fan and a dehumidifier in finished basements. Fresh sealcoat means tar tracked in from driveways.

July

Odor season

Warm humid air makes anything organic in the pad noticeable again. Old pet accidents announce themselves now, months after the visible stain went away.

August

Peak moving season

Turnover cleaning backs up across the metro. Book turnovers two weeks out and clean after the movers, never before.

September

Ragweed

The dominant Midwest fall allergen arrives, along with agricultural dust moving with the harvest. Carpet catches and re-releases both.

October

Second best month

Clean before the house closes up for winter and before holiday traffic. The last easy drying window of the year.

November

Salt returns

First ice-melt applications hit driveways. Put walk-off mats down now, inside and out, at every entry. Schedules fill for the holidays.

December

Traffic spike

The busiest two weeks in the trade. More feet, more spills, more candle wax than any other month. Book well ahead or wait until January.

11Questions

Answers

Fifteen questions,
answered properly

Including the ones where the honest answer costs us the upsell.

Q01How do I know what type of carpet fiber I have?

Pull a few loose strands from a closet edge or a scrap in the utility room and check three things. Nylon feels smooth and springs straight back when you crush it in your fist. Polyester and triexta feel softer and slightly waxy, and they stay flattened a moment longer. Olefin has an almost plastic slickness and is usually found in loop or berber styles and basements. Wool feels warmer and slightly coarse, and it smells like burning hair rather than melting plastic. If your carpet is under about ten years old, the original invoice or the manufacturer sticker inside a closet doorway will tell you exactly. Fiber decides chemistry, so it is the first question we answer on any visit.

Q02Why does the same stain come out of one carpet but not another?

Because fibers accept stains through completely different mechanisms. Standard acid-dyed nylon has open dye sites, which is why the artificial red and blue dyes in sports drinks and children's punch bond so aggressively to it — they are chemically similar to the dye the mill used. Polyester, triexta and olefin rarely take a water-based dye stain at all, but they are oleophilic, meaning they grab oil and grease and hold it. So an identical spill of red punch is a serious problem on nylon and a wipe-up on triexta, while a dropped slice of pizza is the reverse. Same spill, different chemistry, different outcome.

Q03Can carpet cleaning actually help with allergies and asthma?

It helps with the load, not the diagnosis. Carpet behaves like a filter that never gets changed: dust mite fecal particles, pet dander, pollen and fine soil settle into the pile and get re-released into the air every time somebody walks across the room. Hot water extraction removes a large share of that reservoir, and pre-vacuuming removes the dry portion before any water touches it. What cleaning cannot do is cure an allergy or replace a HEPA filter and a humidity strategy. If somebody in the house has asthma, the useful plan is cleaning every three to four months, a real vacuum with a sealed HEPA path, and keeping indoor humidity in the 40 to 50 percent range.

Q04Is it true you should never use hot water on a stain?

It is true for one category and wrong for the others, which is why the blanket advice causes so much damage. Protein — blood, milk, egg, vomit, urine — coagulates with heat exactly the way an egg white sets in a pan, and once it has cooked into the fiber it is effectively permanent. Those get cold water and an enzyme. Tannin and oily soils, on the other hand, release better with warmth. So the rule is not about temperature in the abstract, it is about identifying the stain first. When you do not know what you are looking at, cold is the safe default because cold rarely sets anything.

Q05What is the difference between a spot and a stain?

A spot is a substance sitting on top of the fiber. It is soil, and soil comes out with the right chemistry and extraction. A stain is a dye that has bonded to the fiber or replaced the fiber's own color, and that is not a cleaning problem anymore, it is a color problem. This distinction is the single most useful thing a homeowner can learn, because it explains why a good technician will tell you honestly that three of your four marks will disappear and the fourth one needs a dye correction or a bonded insert. Anyone who promises everything comes out has not looked closely.

Q06Why do old stains come back a few days after cleaning?

That is wicking, and it means the contamination went deeper than the cleaning did. When a spill soaks past the face fiber into the backing or the cushion, cleaning removes only the top portion. As the carpet dries, moisture travels upward by capillary action and carries the remaining material back to the surface with it, so the spot reappears looking like the cleaning failed. It is fixable. The answer is a deeper sub-surface extraction of that specific area rather than another surface pass, and any honest cleaner will come back and do it.

Q07How much soil is actually in a typical carpet?

More than people expect, and most of it is dry. Industry training figures put roughly three-quarters of carpet soil in the dry particulate category — grit, sand, silt, skin cells, fiber fragments — with the remainder being oily and water-soluble residues that bind the dry material in place. This ratio is the entire argument for thorough vacuuming before any wet cleaning. Water applied to dry particulate soil does not remove it, it makes mud out of it and drives it toward the backing where the wand has a much harder time recovering it.

Q08Does the vinegar and baking soda trick work on carpet?

The fizzing is a neutralization reaction between an acid and a base, and once it stops you are left with saltwater and a residue of sodium acetate in your carpet. It is theatre. Vinegar on its own has genuine uses — it is mildly acidic, so it helps on some tannin marks and on alkaline residue — and baking soda on its own absorbs moisture and odor. Combined, they cancel each other out and leave behind a powder that attracts soil. If you want one household item on standby, make it a stack of clean white towels and cool water.

Q09Is oxygen bleach like OxiClean safe to use on my carpet?

On most synthetics, usually, with a test first. On wool, no. The professional shorthand is oxidize organics, reduce synthetics: oxygen-based products break the color bonds in organic material like coffee, tea, wine and juice, while artificial dyes respond better to a reducing agent instead. The risks are that peroxide can lighten some carpet dyes, particularly older or lower-quality ones, and that wool is a protein fiber that reacts badly to high alkalinity. Always test a hidden area, never mix products, and if you are unsure what your carpet is, treat it as wool until you know.

Q10How do I clean carpet in an Omaha basement without creating a mold problem?

Control the drying, not the cleaning. Below-grade rooms in this metro sit against cool foundation walls with limited airflow, so the same amount of moisture that dries in eight hours upstairs can sit for twice that downstairs. The practical version is to clean with lower moisture, run a dehumidifier rather than opening a window, keep air movers going until the carpet is dry to the touch well past the surface, and never put area rugs or boxes back down until the pile is fully dry underneath. If the basement has ever taken water from a storm, say so before we start, because that changes the plan entirely.

Q11Should I clean or replace carpet that is ten years old?

Clean it first, then decide, because cleaning answers the question for free. Dull, flattened, grey traffic lanes are usually soil and matting, and both respond. What does not respond is wear: when the fiber tips have been abraded away, the pile has lost its twist, or the backing has delaminated, no cleaning restores that, because the material itself is gone. A useful test is to look at a protected area under a sofa and compare it to the lane. If the hidden carpet looks new and the lane looks tired, you have a soil problem. If they look the same and both look tired, you have a wear problem.

Q12Do carpet protectors like Scotchgard actually do anything?

Yes, but not what the name suggests. Protector does not repel stains so much as buy you time: it raises the surface tension of the fiber so a spill beads and sits for a few minutes instead of wicking straight into the pile, which converts a lot of would-be stains into blot-ups. It also helps dry soil release more easily during the next cleaning. What it does not do is make carpet stain-proof, and it wears off with traffic and cleaning, which is why it is reapplied after extraction rather than once at installation. It is most worth the money on light-colored carpet, entry lanes and stairs, and least worth it in a low-traffic guest bedroom.

Q13How long does a cleaning take, and do I need to be home?

Budget roughly twenty to twenty-five minutes per room plus setup, so a typical four-room Omaha house runs about two hours. You need to be there at the start for the walkthrough, since that is when we identify fiber, test colorfastness and agree on what happens to each problem area, and at the end for the final walk. The middle is just work, and plenty of clients leave for an errand. What we do need is access to a driveway or curb for the hose run and a clear path to the rooms.

Q14Do you work with landlords and property managers on scheduling?

Yes, and turnover work is a large share of what we do across the metro. Empty units clean faster and cost less than occupied ones, so the ideal sequence is movers out, then cleaning, then the walkthrough. For portfolios we hold recurring slots and issue one itemized invoice per unit recording rooms cleaned and the method used, which is the documentation that resolves deposit disputes and satisfies carpet warranty requirements. Tell us the number of units and your turn schedule and we will build the calendar around it.

Q15Are the products you use safe around kids, pets, and someone with asthma?

The chemistry matters less than the rinse. Almost any professional pre-spray is safe once it is properly extracted; what causes irritation is residue left in the pile because the rinse step was rushed. We finish on an acid-side rinse that returns the fiber to a neutral pH, so what remains in the carpet is carpet. For households with asthma or chemical sensitivity we can run a fragrance-free program and skip deodorizers entirely, which is worth asking for, because scent is almost always added for the customer's benefit rather than the carpet's.
